# Module 4: Use Playbooks to Respond to Incidents

## Key Concepts

### What playbooks are
A **playbook** is a manual providing details about any operational action, essentially a predefined, up-to-date list of steps to follow when responding to an incident. Playbooks matter because urgency, efficiency, and accuracy are all needed to catch and reduce a security threat quickly, and they ensure everyone follows a consistent process regardless of who's handling the case.

- Each playbook comes with a **strategy** (expectations for whoever is assigned a task, sometimes naming specific responsible individuals) and a **plan** (how the task should actually be completed).
- Playbooks are **living documents**, they get updated often as a collaborative effort across the team when a failure/oversight is found, industry standards or regulations change, or threat actor tactics evolve.
- **Types of playbooks**: incident response, security alerts, team-specific, and product-specific. Some are built around specific attack types (ransomware, vishing, BEC, etc.).
- Content/procedures in a playbook can vary by country, since government-imposed incident notification laws/regulations affect what must be included, and requirements can change depending on where the incident happened and what type of data was affected.

### Incident and vulnerability response playbooks
Commonly used by entry-level analysts, and built around an organization's **business continuity plan** (the established path for a business to keep operating despite a disruption like a breach). Since risk roughly equals the likelihood of a threat, urgency matters, and following the defined steps carefully also protects forensic data from being mishandled/rendered unusable.

### The 6 phases of an incident response playbook
1. **Preparation**: documenting procedures, setting staffing plans, and educating users ahead of time. Sets the foundation for the rest of the response (e.g. writing incident response plans that define each team member's role).
2. **Detection and analysis**: using defined processes/technology to detect events and analyze whether a breach occurred and how big it might be.
3. **Containment**: taking action to stop further damage and reduce the immediate impact, a high priority since it protects critical assets from ongoing risk.
4. **Eradication and recovery**: fully removing the incident's artifacts (malicious code, remaining vulnerabilities) and restoring the affected environment to a secure state (also called IT restoration).
5. **Post-incident activity**: documenting the incident, informing leadership, and applying lessons learned; may include a full-scale root-cause analysis and security improvements depending on severity.
6. **Coordination**: reporting the incident and sharing info throughout the response process according to the org's standards, this supports compliance and a coordinated resolution.

### How SIEM tools and playbooks work together
SIEM tools collect/analyze data and generate alerts; playbooks then guide the analyst's response once an alert comes in. Example walkthrough for a malware alert:
1. **Assess the alert**: confirm it's valid by checking why the SIEM generated it (log data, related metrics).
2. **Contain**: follow the playbook's instructions to isolate/disconnect the infected system so malware can't spread further.
3. **Eradicate and restore**: remove all traces of the incident and restore affected systems, e.g. reinstalling the OS and restoring data from a clean backup made before the outbreak.
4. **Post-incident and coordination**: create a final report for stakeholders, and report to relevant authorities (e.g. the FBI) if needed.

### Playbooks and SOAR tools
**SOAR (Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response)** tools are similar to SIEM tools in that they support threat monitoring, but SOAR specifically automates repetitive tasks generated by tools like SIEM or MDR (managed detection and response). Example: if a user fails too many login attempts, a SOAR tool can automatically lock the account, and the analyst then follows a playbook to resolve the underlying issue. Playbooks (sometimes also called **runbooks**) are what tell the team exactly who needs to do what and when.

### Why diversity of perspective matters on security teams
People bring different life experience and ways of seeing a problem, someone with a journalism or entertainment background, for example, might flag concerns a purely technical reviewer would miss. Since products and protections are built for a wide range of people, having varied perspectives in the room helps teams catch blind spots and build more effective, trustworthy protections.
