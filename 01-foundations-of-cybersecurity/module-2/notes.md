# Module 2: Foundations of Cybersecurity: The Evolution of Cybersecurity

## Key Concepts

### Past attacks that shaped the industry
- **Brain virus (1986)**: Created by the Alvi brothers to track illegal copies of medical software. Spread accidentally through infected floppy disks and ended up slowing down business operations worldwide. Showed the industry it needed a real plan to protect data and productivity.
- **Morris worm (1988)**: Built by Robert Morris to count how many computers were connected to the internet. A bug caused it to keep reinstalling itself, crashing around 6,000 machines (about 10% of the internet at the time). Led to the creation of CERTs (Computer Emergency Response Teams) to respond to security incidents, which still exist today.
- **LoveLetter attack (2000)**: Malware disguised as an email titled "I Love You." Once opened, it scanned the victim's contacts and auto-forwarded itself while stealing login credentials. Infected around 45 million computers and caused over $10 billion in damages. Considered the first major example of social engineering.
- **Equifax breach (2017)**: Attackers exploited multiple unpatched vulnerabilities to steal over 143 million customer records (SSNs, birth dates, driver's license numbers, addresses, credit card numbers), affecting about 40% of Americans. Equifax paid over $575 million in settlements, which pushed many companies to take breach prevention more seriously.

### Common attack categories
- **Phishing**: Tricking people into revealing sensitive data or installing malware through digital communication.
  - *Business Email Compromise (BEC)*: impersonating a known source by email to gain a financial advantage.
  - *Spear phishing*: targeted email attack on a specific person/group, appears to come from a trusted source.
  - *Whaling*: spear phishing aimed specifically at executives.
  - *Vishing*: same idea but over voice calls.
  - *Smishing*: same idea but over text messages.
- **Malware**: software built to harm devices/networks, usually for money or intelligence advantage.
  - *Virus*: needs a user to open an infected file/attachment to activate.
  - *Worm*: spreads on its own across a network without needing a user to open anything.
  - *Ransomware*: encrypts an organization's data and demands payment to unlock it.
  - *Spyware*: secretly collects and sells personal data (emails, texts, recordings, location).
- **Social engineering**: exploiting human trust rather than technical flaws to get access or information.
  - *Social media phishing*: gathering info about a target from their social profiles before attacking.
  - *Watering hole attack*: compromising a site the target group visits often.
  - *USB baiting*: leaving an infected USB drive somewhere an employee will find and plug it in.
  - *Physical social engineering*: impersonating an employee/vendor to get physical access to a location.
  - Why it works so well: people tend to respect authority, follow social proof, act fast under urgency, or trust a "familiar" connection. Threat actors lean on those instincts (authority, intimidation, consensus, scarcity, familiarity, trust, urgency).
- **Other attack types**: password attacks (brute force, rainbow table), physical attacks (malicious USB/cable, card skimming), cryptographic attacks (birthday, collision, downgrade), supply-chain attacks (hitting a vulnerable link in a vendor/production chain), and adversarial AI (manipulating AI/ML systems to attack more efficiently).

### The 8 CISSP security domains
A way of organizing the different responsibilities of a security team. Gaps in any one domain can put the whole organization at risk.
1. **Security and risk management**: setting security goals, handling compliance (e.g. HIPAA), risk mitigation, business continuity.
2. **Asset security**: securing and properly disposing of digital/physical assets and data.
3. **Security architecture and engineering**: optimizing tools/systems, e.g. configuring firewalls.
4. **Communication and network security**: securing physical networks and wireless communication, e.g. policies against unsecured Wi-Fi use.
5. **Identity and access management**: controlling who can access physical and digital assets (e.g. keycards, account permissions).
6. **Security assessment and testing**: auditing systems and user permissions to catch risks/vulnerabilities.
7. **Security operations**: day-to-day investigation and response to active threats.
8. **Software development security**: applying secure coding practices throughout the software development lifecycle.

### Threat actor types
- **Advanced persistent threats (APTs)**: highly skilled, research targets in advance, can stay hidden in a network for a long time. Often go after critical infrastructure or intellectual property.
- **Insider threats**: people who already have authorized access and misuse it (sabotage, corruption, espionage, leaks).
- **Hacktivists**: politically motivated, use hacking for demonstrations, propaganda, or social change campaigns.

### Hacker types
- **Authorized (ethical) hackers**: follow the law/code of ethics, work to protect organizations.
- **Semi-authorized hackers**: researchers who find vulnerabilities but don't exploit them.
- **Unauthorized (unethical) hackers**: malicious actors who break the law, usually for financial gain.
- Some hackers work contract jobs without a strong personal agenda either way, and some consider themselves "vigilantes" trying to stop unethical hackers.

## New Terms (Glossary)

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Adversarial AI | Manipulating AI/ML technology to carry out attacks more efficiently |
| Business Email Compromise (BEC) | Phishing where an attacker impersonates a known source for financial gain |
| CISSP | Certified Information Systems Security Professional, a globally recognized security certification |
| Computer virus | Malicious code that interferes with computer operations and damages data/software |
| Cryptographic attack | An attack targeting secure communication between sender and recipient |
| Hacker | Anyone who uses computers to gain access to systems, networks, or data |
| Malware | Software designed to harm devices or networks |
| Password attack | An attempt to break into password-protected devices, systems, or data |
| Phishing | Using digital communication to trick people into revealing data or installing malware |
| Physical attack | A security incident affecting physical, not just digital, environments |
| Physical social engineering | Impersonating an employee/customer/vendor to gain unauthorized physical access |
| Social engineering | Manipulation technique that exploits human error to gain info, access, or valuables |
| Social media phishing | Gathering info about a target from social media before attacking |
| Spear phishing | Targeted email attack on a specific person/group, appearing to be from a trusted source |
| Supply-chain attack | Targeting a vulnerability anywhere across a product/service's supply chain |
| USB baiting | Leaving an infected USB drive for someone to find and plug in |
| Vishing | Voice-based phishing |
| Watering hole attack | Compromising a site that a specific group of users visits often |
