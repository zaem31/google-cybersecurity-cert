# Module 3: Foundations of Cybersecurity: Protect Against Threats, Risks and Vulnerabilities

## Key Concepts

### Security frameworks and controls
- **Security frameworks**: guidelines for building a plan to reduce risk to data and privacy. They give structure to the "security lifecycle," the ongoing set of policies that define how an org manages risk and meets compliance.
- **4 core components of a framework**:
  1. Identify and document security goals.
  2. Set guidelines to reach those goals.
  3. Implement strong security processes.
  4. Monitor and communicate results.
- **Security controls**: specific safeguards used to reduce a specific risk (e.g. requiring all staff to complete privacy training, then tracking who completed it).
- **Compliance**: the process of following both internal standards and external regulations.

### The CIA triad
A foundational model for thinking about risk when designing systems and policies:
- **Confidentiality**: only authorized users can access specific data/assets (enforced through access controls).
- **Integrity**: data is accurate, authentic, and hasn't been tampered with (often protected via encryption).
- **Availability**: data is accessible to the people who are authorized to use it.
- **Asset**: anything an organization considers valuable, valued based on what it would cost to lose or have compromised.

### NIST Cybersecurity Framework (NIST CSF)
A voluntary framework from the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, made up of standards, guidelines, and best practices used as a baseline for managing cybersecurity risk. NIST also has a Risk Management Framework (RMF) for a similar purpose.

### Other major frameworks, controls & compliance standards
- **FERC-NERC**: applies to organizations tied to the U.S./North American power grid; requires preparing for, mitigating, and reporting incidents that could affect the grid.
- **FedRAMP**: standardizes how the U.S. federal government assesses, authorizes, and monitors cloud services.
- **CIS (Center for Internet Security)**: a nonprofit providing practical controls organizations can follow to defend systems/networks and respond to incidents.
- **GDPR**: EU regulation protecting the personal data and privacy of EU residents. Breach victims must be notified within 72 hours; non-transparency about data use can result in fines.
- **PCI DSS**: international standard for securely handling credit card data, aimed at reducing credit card fraud.
- **HIPAA**: U.S. federal law (1996) protecting patient health information (PHI). Governed by three rules: privacy, security, and breach notification. HITRUST is a framework that helps organizations meet HIPAA compliance.
- **ISO**: sets international standards for technology, manufacturing, and management practices across countries.
- **SOC 1 / SOC 2**: reports (developed by AICPA) that assess an organization's user-access policies and overall data safety across roles like associate, supervisor, manager, executive, and vendor. Failures here can lead to fraud.
- Other regulations worth knowing exist and change often, e.g. the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act and Sarbanes-Oxley Act.

### Ethics in cybersecurity
- **Security ethics**: guidelines for making the right call as a security professional, especially since security teams often have more access to sensitive data than most employees, which comes with responsibility.
- **3 ethical principles that come up often**:
  1. **Confidentiality**: never share private/proprietary data outside proper channels, even to help a coworker.
  2. **Privacy protection**: safeguarding personal info from unauthorized use, e.g. refusing to share an employee's private info outside official/secure channels even under pressure.
  3. **The law**: rules enforced by a governing body; negligently mishandling protected data (like leaving PII/SPII files unsupervised) can be both unethical and illegal.
- **Counterattacks**:
  - In the **U.S.**, counterattacking a threat actor is illegal (Computer Fraud and Abuse Act 1986, Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act 2015). Only approved government/military personnel may do it; everyone else is limited to defense only.
  - **Internationally**, the International Court of Justice allows a counterattack only if it targets just the original attacker, is a direct request to stop, doesn't escalate the situation, and its effects can be reversed. In practice, most organizations avoid counterattacking since these conditions are hard to guarantee and mistakes can make things worse.

## New Terms (Glossary)

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Asset | Anything perceived as having value to an organization |
| Availability | Data is accessible to those authorized to access it |
| Compliance | Adhering to internal standards and external regulations |
| Confidentiality | Only authorized users can access specific assets or data |
| CIA triad | Model for evaluating risk around confidentiality, integrity, and availability |
| Hacktivist | Someone who hacks to achieve a political goal |
| HIPAA | U.S. federal law protecting patients' health information |
| Integrity | Data is correct, authentic, and reliable |
| NIST CSF | Voluntary framework of standards/guidelines/best practices for managing cybersecurity risk |
| Privacy protection | Safeguarding personal information from unauthorized use |
| PHI (Protected Health Information) | Info relating to an individual's past, present, or future physical/mental health |
| Security architecture | A security design made of tools/processes used to protect an org from risk |
| Security controls | Safeguards designed to reduce specific security risks |
| Security ethics | Guidelines for making appropriate decisions as a security professional |
| Security frameworks | Guidelines for building a plan to mitigate risk to data/privacy |
| Security governance | Practices that support, define, and direct an org's security efforts |
| SPII | A stricter subset of PII requiring tighter handling |
