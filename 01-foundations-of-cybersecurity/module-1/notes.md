# Module 1: Foundations of Cybersecurity:Introduction to Cybersecurity

## Key Concepts

- **What security does**: Security teams protect an organization's networks, devices, people, and data, and respond quickly when something does get through, similar to patching leaks during a storm.
- **External vs. internal threats**:
  - *External threat*: someone outside the organization trying to access private data, networks, or devices.
  - *Internal threat*: comes from current/former employees, vendors, or partners. Can be accidental (e.g. clicking a phishing link) or intentional (e.g. misusing access).
- **Why security matters to a business** (4 main reasons):
  1. Meets regulatory compliance: avoids fines/audits and fulfills ethical duty to protect users.
  2. Maintains business continuity: lets the company keep operating even during an incident like a breach.
  3. Reduces costs tied to incidents (data loss, downtime, fines).
  4. Protects brand trust: a breach damages reputation and can cost the business revenue long-term.
- **Common entry-level job titles**: Security Analyst/Specialist, Cybersecurity Analyst/Specialist, SOC (Security Operation Center) Analyst, Information Security Analyst.
- **Core responsibilities of an entry-level analyst**:
  1. Protect computer/network systems: monitor networks, respond to threats, and take part in penetration testing (ethically "hacking" their own org to find weak spots).
  2. Prevent threats proactively: work with IT to install prevention software and support secure product/software development.
  3. Conduct periodic security audits: review records/activities (e.g. checking that passwords aren't openly accessible to everyone).
- **Analyst vs. Engineer** (from the interview with Nikki): an analyst leans more toward day-to-day operations/investigations; an engineer also builds and improves detection systems (more project-based work).
- **Blue team vs. red team**: Blue team defends systems; red team simulates attacks to find weaknesses.
- **PII vs. SPII**:
  - *PII (Personally Identifiable Information)*: data that can identify a person, such as full name, birth date, address, phone number, email, or IP address.
  - *SPII (Sensitive PII)*: a stricter subset of PII, such as SSNs, financial/medical info, and biometric data. More damaging if stolen.
- **Identity theft**: stealing someone's personal info to commit fraud, usually for financial gain.
- **Industry outlook**: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects security-role demand to grow 30%+ by 2030.

## New Terms (Glossary)

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Cybersecurity (Security) | Practice of ensuring confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information by protecting networks, devices, people, and data from unauthorized access/exploitation |
| Cloud security | Ensuring cloud-stored assets are properly configured and access is limited to authorized users |
| Internal threat | A current/former employee, vendor, or partner who poses a security risk |
| Network security | Keeping an organization's network infrastructure secure from unauthorized access |
| PII | Any info used to infer someone's identity |
| SPII | A stricter subset of PII (SSN, medical, financial, biometric data) |
| Security posture | An org's ability to manage defense of assets/data and respond to change |
| Threat | Any circumstance/event that could negatively impact assets |
| Threat actor | Any person or group presenting a security risk |
| Technical skills | Skills requiring knowledge of specific tools, procedures, policies |
| Transferable skills | Skills from other fields that apply to a new career |
| Compliance | Adhering to internal standards + external regulations |
| Security framework | Guidelines for building plans to mitigate risk |
| Security controls | Safeguards that reduce specific security risks |

## Transferable Skills (from other life/work experience)

- Communication (explaining issues to technical & non-technical people)
- Problem-solving (spotting attack patterns, accepting imperfect trade-off solutions)
- Time management (prioritizing urgent issues)
- Growth mindset (willingness to keep learning as tech evolves)
- Valuing diverse perspectives (better solutions through team collaboration)

## Technical Skills to Build

- Programming (e.g. Python, SQL): automate repetitive tasks like scanning for malicious domains
- SIEM tools (Security Information and Event Management): collect/analyze log data to spot threats
- Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS): monitor for unauthorized/suspicious activity
- Computer forensics: identify, analyze, and preserve digital evidence after an incident

## Real-World Perspectives (from Google security pros)

- **Toni** (Security Engineering Manager): came from a non-technical background, learned Python/SQL and cybersecurity vocabulary on the job. Key advice: stay curious, imposter syndrome is common, so push through it instead of giving up.
- **Nikki** (Security Engineer, insider threat team): entry-level day-to-day = mix of operations (investigating detections) and project work (building/improving detections). Highlighted the value of writing clear playbooks for the team.
- **Veronica** (Security Engineer): started with zero cybersecurity background via an IT residency; proof that a college degree isn't required to succeed in the field. Advice: don't wait to feel "expert enough" to pursue opportunities.
